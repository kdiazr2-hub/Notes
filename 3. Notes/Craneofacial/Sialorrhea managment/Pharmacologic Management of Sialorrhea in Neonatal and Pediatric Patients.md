## Some of the scoring methods used to evaluate sialorrhea are: 

* DIS:  From 1 to 10

The DIS typically measures the following dimensions over the preceding week:

1. Frequency of dribbling
2. Severity of the drooling
3. Number of times clothing/bibs require changing
4. Offensiveness of the saliva's smell
5. Presence of skin irritation/rashes
6. Frequency of mouth wiping
7. Level of embarrassment experienced by the patient
8. Impact on social situations/activities
9. Effect on speech clarity
10. Overall disruption to the caregiver's or family's life

* TDS: 

1= No drooling
2= Infrequent drooling, small amount
3= Occasional drooling on and off all day
4= Frequent drooling, but not profuse
5= Constant drooling, always wet

# Pharmacologyc agents

## Atropine: 

![[Pasted image 20260721204635.png|256]]

Atropine is an antimuscarinic (anticholinergic) drug. It blocks acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors, decreasing salivary-gland secretions.

For pediatric sialorrhea, a commonly used off-label regimen is:

Atropine 1% ophthalmic solution (10 mg/mL): start with 1 drop sublingually once daily. 

Some pediatric protocols allow 1–2 drops every 4–6 hours as needed. One standard 0.05-mL drop contains approximately 0.5 mg, although drop size can vary.

Atropine is a competitive, reversible antagonist of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors.

*Mechanism of action*

For sialorrhea, it mainly blocks M3 receptors in the salivary glands, preventing parasympathetic acetylcholine from stimulating saliva secretion. This reduces the volume of saliva and makes oral secretions drier.

When administered sublingually:

* It acts on receptors in the mouth and is also absorbed systemically.

* It reduces saliva production, but does not correct the underlying swallowing or oral-motor dysfunction.

* Because atropine crosses the blood–brain barrier, excessive doses can cause central effects such as agitation, confusion, or hallucinations.

> [!danger] Other clinical adverse effects include xerostomia, behavioral changes.


## Glyopirrolate: 

![[Pasted image 20260721204734.png|162]]

Glycopyrrolate—also called glycopyrronium—is a peripheral antimuscarinic medication used to reduce chronic severe drooling in children with neurological conditions such as cerebral palsy.

*Pediatric dose for sialorrhea*

For children 3–16 years, using glycopyrrolate oral solution 1 mg/5 mL (0.2 mg/mL):

Initial dose: 0.02 mg/kg orally three times daily.
Increase by 0.02 mg/kg per dose every 5–7 days, according to effectiveness and adverse effects.
Maximum: 0.1 mg/kg per dose three times daily, subject to these weight-based limits:
 
| Weight   | Maximum per dose |
| -------- | ---------------: |
| 13–17 kg |           1.5 mg |
| 18–22 kg |             2 mg |
| 23–27 kg |           2.5 mg |
| ≥28 kg   |             3 mg |

Administer one hour before or two hours after meals, because high-fat food substantially decreases absorption. Safety and efficacy have not been established in children under three years.

*Mechanism of action*

Glycopyrrolate is a competitive antagonist of peripheral muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. By blocking acetylcholine—particularly at receptors in the salivary glands—it prevents parasympathetic stimulation and reduces saliva production.

Unlike atropine, glycopyrrolate is a quaternary ammonium compound that crosses the blood–brain barrier poorly. Therefore, it generally causes fewer central effects such as confusion, agitation, or hallucinations. It reduces secretion production but does not correct the underlying swallowing dysfunction.


> [!danger]  *Important adverse effects:* 
> - Dry mouth
>- Constipation or intestinal pseudo-obstruction
>- Vomiting
>- Urinary retention
>- Flushing and reduced sweating, with risk of overheating
>- Increased viscosity of bronchial secretions
>- Tachycardia or blurred vision

Use cautiously in renal impairment and avoid it in conditions such as glaucoma, paralytic ileus, myasthenia gravis, severe ulcerative colitis, or toxic megacolon.

## Scopolamine:

![[Pasted image 20260721204828.png|395]]
Scopolamine, also called hyoscine hydrobromide, is an antimuscarinic medication used off-label to reduce severe drooling in children with neurological disorders. It is usually administered through a transdermal patch. It should not be confused with hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan).

Pediatric dose for sialorrhea

A commonly used specialist regimen with a 1.5 mg patch that releases approximately 1 mg over 72 hours is:

| Age           | Delivered dose | Frequency      |
| ------------- | -------------: | -------------- |
| Under 3 years | 250 micrograms | Every 72 hours |
| 3–9 years     | 500 micrograms | Every 72 hours |
| ≥10 years     |           1 mg | Every 72 hours |

Apply to clean, dry, hairless skin—usually behind the ear—and alternate sides when replacing it. Treatment of pediatric sialorrhea is off-label.

*Mechanism of action*

Scopolamine is a competitive antagonist of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. It blocks parasympathetic stimulation—particularly at M3 receptors in the salivary glands—thereby reducing saliva production.

Unlike glycopyrrolate, scopolamine is a tertiary amine that readily crosses the blood–brain barrier. It can therefore produce more central nervous system effects, including drowsiness, confusion, agitation, and hallucinations.

>[!danger] Important adverse effects
>- Dry mouth and thickened respiratory secretions
>- Constipation and urinary retention
>- Blurred vision or pupil dilation, especially after eye contamination
>- Tachycardia
>- Drowsiness, dizziness or confusion
>- Agitation, hallucinations or acute psychosis
>- Reduced sweating, fever and potentially serious hyperthermia
>- Local skin irritation
