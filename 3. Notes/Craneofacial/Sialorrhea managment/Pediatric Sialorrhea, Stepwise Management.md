
> [!abstract] Bottom line  
> Pediatric sialorrhea should be managed with an individualized, severity-informed, multidisciplinary pathway. Begin with clinical risk stratification, use behavioral/oral-motor therapy for low-risk mild disease, escalate to anticholinergics when symptoms persist, use botulinum toxin as a reversible option for refractory disease or medication intolerance, and reserve surgery for severe, persistent, or refractory cases. Red flags should shorten or bypass the conservative pathway.

## Clinical context

![[Pasted image 20260721205746.png|260]]

- Sialorrhea is the involuntary loss of saliva. Persistence beyond 4 years of age is considered abnormal.
    
- It is common in children with neurological or motor impairment, especially cerebral palsy.
    
- The main mechanism is often impaired oral and pharyngeal motor control with ineffective swallowing rather than true salivary hypersecretion.
    
- Consequences include perioral skin injury, halitosis, aspiration and recurrent respiratory infections, clothing changes, social stigma, reduced participation and self-esteem, and substantial caregiver burden.
    

## Evidence base

- PRISMA-guided systematic review; PROSPERO **CRD420251231755**.
    
- **22 studies; 1,028 pediatric participants.**
    
- Only randomized controlled trials were included for non-surgical treatments; all study designs were eligible for surgery because randomized surgical evidence was limited.
    
- Outcomes included drooling severity/frequency, adverse events, durability, function, and caregiver-reported impact.
    
- Evidence was assessed with Cochrane RoB 2, MINORS, and GRADE.
    

## Initial assessment

Assess:

1. **Pattern:** anterior drooling versus posterior drooling/aspiration.
    
2. **Severity and frequency:** preferably with the same validated instrument over time, such as the Drooling Severity and Frequency Scale (DSFS), Drooling Quotient, or Drooling Impact Scale.
    
3. **Swallowing and airway safety:** dysphagia, aspiration, pneumonia, recurrent lower respiratory tract infections, chronic lung disease, or airway compromise.
    
4. **Nutrition and growth:** feeding difficulty or failure to thrive attributable to drooling.
    
5. **Neurological course:** stable/non-progressive versus progressive disease; oral-motor ability and head/postural control.
    
6. **Functional burden:** skin breakdown, communication and social participation, school impact, caregiver workload, and family priorities.
    
7. **Previous treatment:** response, adherence, intolerance, contraindications, and whether the child can participate in behavioral therapy.
    

> [!danger] Red flags requiring accelerated escalation
> 
> - Aspiration pneumonia or recurrent chest infections
>     
> - Chronic lung disease related to aspiration
>     
> - Failure to thrive attributable to drooling
>     
> - Continuous/uncontrolled drooling with airway compromise
>     
> - Severe skin breakdown, social exclusion, or severe caregiver burden
>     
> - Documented failure of prior conservative treatment
>     
> - Contraindication or poor tolerance to anticholinergics
>     
> - Inability to cooperate with behavioral therapy
>     

## Stepwise treatment algorithm

### 1. Behavioral and rehabilitative therapy

**Role:** first-line treatment for low-risk, usually mild disease.

- Oral-motor therapy (OMT) was the most frequently studied conservative intervention.
    
- Other approaches included functional chewing training, positioning/postural strategies, behavioral reinforcement, and Kinesio taping as an adjunct.
    
- OMT, particularly when paired with supervised caregiver participation, produced modest reductions in drooling severity and frequency.
    
- Kinesio taping may improve short-term outcomes, but sustained benefit is uncertain.
    

**Evidence:** 4 studies, n = 68; low-to-moderate certainty; conditional recommendation; benefit mainly over **2–12 weeks**.

**Escalate if:** there is no clinically meaningful reduction in severity or frequency.

### 2. [[Pharmacologic Management of Sialorrhea in Neonatal and Pediatric Patients]]: anticholinergics

**Role:** second-line therapy for persistent symptoms, moderate disease, or non-response to behavioral treatment.

- Glycopyrrolate/glycopyrronium and hyoscine (scopolamine) reduced drooling.
    
- Glycopyrronium showed meaningful improvement in severe sialorrhea and reduced its effect on children and families.
    
- Hyoscine and glycopyrronium were both effective in one comparative trial, but hyoscine caused more troublesome adverse effects and more treatment discontinuation.
    
- In one glycopyrrolate trial, approximately **20%** of children developed adverse effects substantial enough to stop treatment.
    

**Evidence:** 3 studies, n = 216; moderate certainty; medium-term benefit; conditional-to-strong recommendation depending on the clinical context.

**Escalate if:** benefit is inadequate, adverse effects are unacceptable, or anticholinergics are contraindicated.

> [!caution]  
> Anticholinergic choice and titration must be individualized. The review supports efficacy but emphasizes systemic adverse effects and treatment intolerance rather than a single universally preferred regimen.

### 3. Botulinum toxin injection

**Role:** third-line, reversible, minimally invasive treatment for refractory disease, medication intolerance, or when temporary control is preferable to surgery.

- Injections reduce salivary output for weeks to months, but repeat treatment is often required.
    
- Ultrasound-guided intraglandular injection is preferred, particularly for the submandibular glands; the parotid and/or submandibular glands may be targeted.
    
- Expected kinetics described in the review: onset at about **1 week**, peak effect near **1 month**, and duration about **3–4 months**.
    
- Consensus guidance cited by the authors advises against repeating injections at intervals shorter than **3 months**.
    
- Reported total botulinum toxin type A doses ranged from **30 to 100 units**, depending on body weight, gland selection, and formulation. These values are descriptive and are not interchangeable across toxin products.
    

**Potential adverse effects:** xerostomia, thickened secretions, transient worsening of swallowing, parotitis, and local injection-site complications. Children with significant dysphagia require careful selection and close follow-up.

**Evidence used in the algorithm:** 2 small studies, n = 30; low certainty; short-to-medium-term benefit; conditional recommendation.

**Aftercare:** early observation, close review during the first week, dietary modification when indicated, and reassessment of swallowing if symptoms change.

### 4. Surgery

**Role:** definitive last-line treatment for severe, persistent, or refractory sialorrhea, or when repeated injections are ineffective, unsuitable, or undesirable.

- Procedures included submandibular duct ligation, duct relocation, submandibular gland excision, and multi-duct ligation.
    
- Surgery produced the largest and most durable reduction in drooling, but with greater morbidity and a need for careful multidisciplinary selection.
    
- Bilateral submandibular duct ligation was more effective and longer-lasting than botulinum toxin in comparative trials, although complications and morbidity were slightly greater.
    

**Procedure selection by phenotype:**

- **Predominantly anterior, non-progressive drooling with preserved swallowing:** favor salivary diversion such as submandibular duct relocation.
    
- **Posterior drooling, aspiration risk, or progressive neurological disease:** consider more definitive salivary-reduction procedures such as two-/three-duct or multi-duct ligation, with submandibular gland excision in selected patients.
    

**Evidence:** 7 studies, n = 405; moderate certainty; largest effect and durability up to 32 weeks in the algorithm; strong recommendation in selected patients.

## Red-flag pathways

- **Aspiration-dominant or life-threatening disease:** bypass prolonged behavioral and medication trials. Botulinum toxin is often preferred first when reversible control is needed or surgical risk/prognosis is uncertain; proceed to surgery for severe persistent disease, failed/unsuitable botulinum toxin, or need for durable control.
    
- **Major functional or social burden without immediate airway threat:** pharmacologic therapy or botulinum toxin; surgery after failure of less invasive options.
    
- **Treatment-related limitations:** skip any contraindicated or impractical step and choose the safest effective alternative.
    

## Multidisciplinary care

Management may involve speech and language therapy, pediatric neurology, otolaryngology, dentistry/oral surgery, rehabilitation, and pediatric surgery. Shared decision-making should incorporate aspiration risk, treatment reversibility, expected duration, adverse effects, the child's ability to participate, and family priorities.

## Critical appraisal

**Strengths**

- Broad systematic synthesis across behavioral, medication, injection, and surgical modalities.
    
- Explicit integration of severity, durability, invasiveness, GRADE certainty, and escalation criteria.
    
- Clinically useful distinction between routine stepwise care and accelerated red-flag pathways.
    

**Limitations**

- Considerable heterogeneity in interventions, outcome measures, follow-up, and dosing.
    
- Overall randomized-trial risk of bias was moderate, especially because of limited blinding, attrition, and underreported allocation concealment.
    
- Botulinum toxin evidence informing the algorithm was based on very small trials.
    
- Much of the surgical evidence came from non-randomized cohorts.
    
- Long-term and patient-/caregiver-centered outcomes were inconsistently reported.
    
- The proposed algorithm still requires prospective validation.
    

## Key takeaways

1. Treat the child and the burden of drooling, not the saliva measurement alone.
    
2. Screen for aspiration, airway compromise, growth failure, dysphagia, and neurological progression before starting a routine conservative pathway.
    
3. Use the least invasive effective option, but do not prolong ineffective conservative therapy when red flags are present.
    
4. Reassess with the same outcome measure after each intervention and escalate for inadequate response, intolerance, or short-lived benefit.
    
5. Choose surgery according to anterior/posterior phenotype, swallowing safety, disease trajectory, and family goals—not severity alone.
