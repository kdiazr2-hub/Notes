Preface
Cleft lip and palate is one of the most common congenital deformities. The treatment of clefts and associated
maxillofacial deformities still presents a difficult problem despite major advances made during the past 25
years. Since the prevention of this anomaly seems unlikely in the near future, attention must be focused on
further improvement of the treatment procedures by a multidisciplinary team.
Successful treatment of cleft lip and palate involves different specialists in the various stages of treatment, and each carries different responsibilities. However, the surgeon’s performance is primary, determining the success or failure of the overall treatment. For this reason I believe that the surgeon must be
well-educated, highly skilled, and experienced in dealing with the great variety of cleft deformities. The
surgeon’s level of competence is crucial to the patient’s appearance, function, facial growth, speech, and
self-esteem.
During my 40 years as a plastic and reconstructive surgeon, my primary interest was in the surgical treatment of cleft lip and palate and associated maxillofacial deformities. I was also interested in experimental research on the various factors affecting maxillofacial growth as a result of surgical treatment. During the
past ten years of my career, I concentrated on analyzing the late results of the treatment procedures I had used
over the years. Along with my colleagues on the cleft palate team in the Department of Otolaryngology—
Head and Neck Surgery at The University of Iowa (H.L. Morris, W. Olin, K.M. Kelly, and others), I led a series of studies evaluating our late results. This analysis allowed my colleagues and me to learn about the efficiency of our treatment procedures and to design new strategic plans for the treatment of cleft lip and palate,
as well as new surgical techniques.
Over the years I have written numerous articles and chapters in books as well as having written or coedited
five books on cleft lip and palate. Through this writing I became acquainted with the vast amount of literature on the subject. Although the enormous bibliography gave the impression that procedures used in the
treatment of cleft lip and palate had been thoroughly studied and documented with a high rate of success, upon
closer investigation I discovered that the quantity of publications did not indicate a high quality of treatment
results. In addition, I found very few textbooks that taught the practical aspects of the treatment procedures
and led the surgeon through the various intricacies of the surgical techniques.
With this notion in mind, I joined with my long-time collaborator Dr. Kenneth E. Salyer, whom I consider
to be one of the leading experts in the surgical treatment of clefts, to fill the void in the literature of the field
and to write the First and Second Editions of the Surgical Techniques of Cleft Lip and Palate. Following this
successful endeavor, I coedited two other books on clefts: Multidisciplinary Management of Cleft Lip and
Palate, with Hughlett L. Morris, and Cleft Palate Speech Management: A Multidisciplinary Approach, with
Robert J. Shprintzen.
While writing through the years on the subject of the surgical treatment of cleft lip and palate, the idea
brewed within me of creating a step-by-step color atlas to guide surgeons through the sophisticated procedures involved in repairing the primary and secondary deformities associated with this congenital defect.
When I shared this idea with Dr. Salyer, he responded enthusiastically and we decided to create a unique atlas with two separate volumes—one on craniofacial surgery and the other on cleft lip and palate surgery.
I had the good fortune of working on this volume with Hani Elkadi, who executed the exquisite artwork,
and William Winn, who prepared the sketches for Dr. Salyer, and on collaborating on this volume with leading specialists in the field: Frank E. Åbyholm, Court B. Cutting, Leonard T. Furlow, Jr., M. Samuel
Noordhoff, and James A. Trott.

PRIMARY UNLATERAL CLEFT-LIP/NOSE REPAIR:
SALYER’S TECHNIQUE
I

Kenneth E. Salyer
Twenty-five years ago, I began performing unilateral cleft-lip/nose repair by using the principles of
the Millard procedure. Over the years, I continually modified the technique in an effort to achieve
optimal aesthetic and functional results. I continue to use the rotation–advancement technique
for lip repair, but I have eliminated the transverse
cut at the alar base on the cleft side. My approach
allows easy access to the nasal cartilages through
the incision around the ala on the cleft side. My
modifications include reconstruction of the nasal
floor, straightening of the columella, placement of
the base of the ala in a position symmetric with the
opposite side, and reconstruction of the ala by
changing the shape of the lower lateral cartilage. I

also try to bring the domes to the same level to create a symmetric nasal tip.
My approach is flexible. It may be tailored to suit
the various forms and severity of the cleft. The basic principles and goals of cleft-lip/nose repair
must be clearly determined, but the details of the
surgical procedures may be changed to fit the individual situation.
In unilateral cleft-lip/nose deformity, varying
degrees of displacement and hypoplasia affect the
lip, nose, and bony structures, a fact that must be
taken into account when performing primary
lip/nose repair. My most recent modifications are
presented in the following figures.

PRIMARY UNILATERAL CLEFT-LIP/NOSE REPAIR:
BARDACH’S TECHNIQUE
I

Janusz Bardach

This technique evolved from the basic concept of
triangular flap cleft-lip repair described by Tennison and later refined by Randall. Precise measurements are used to define the dimensions of the
equilateral triangular flap. It is created on the cleft
side and is inserted into an equilateral-triangular
defect on the noncleft side.
I added the following steps to the initial procedure: construction of the nasal floor, creation of a
deep sulcus, closure of nasolabial fistulae, and correction of the nasal deformity. It is essential that
the incisions in the skin correspond precisely with
those on the muscles and mucosa, and that all layers are sutured with the use of the triangular flap,
thus preventing vertical scar contracture. This pro-

cedure lengthens both lip segments and produces
a symmetric, balanced lip with a well-defined Cupid’s bow, a symmetric vermilion, and a properly
aligned orbicularis oris muscle. It also results in
the construction of the nasal floor and contributes
to correction of the nasal deformity.
I do not recommend making incisions in the sulcus or undermining the soft tissue on the face of
the maxilla. I preserve the fibrous bend between
the alveolar ridge at the cleft margin and the lip
segment on the cleft side. This bend is essential in
pulling the lesser maxillary segment into proper
alignment and may substitute for presurgical orthodontic treatment.

CORRECTION OF THE SECONDARY UNILATERAL
CLEFT-LIP AND NASAL DEFORMITIES
I

Janusz Bardach
The most common secondary deformities after
unilateral cleft-lip repair are a notch in the vermilion, vertical shortening of the lip, an asymmetric
vermilion, an uneven Cupid’s bow, and a hypertrophic scar. I do not recommend correcting these
deformities too soon; some of them improve with
time because of lip function and pressure from the
lower lip. In cases of scar contracture or hypertrophic scarring, massage with vitamin E cream is
highly recommended. Quite frequently marked
improvement may be obtained, precluding the
need for more surgery.
Usually I wait ⱖ2 or 3 years before surgically
correcting the existing lip deformity. I commonly
excise the existing scar and repeat the design used
in primary lip repair. In some cases, additional
Z-plasty may be helpful to establish a symmetric
lip and vermilion contour.
Limited correction of the nasal deformity is performed at the time of primary lip repair. However,
in the majority of cases, correction of secondary
nasal deformities is required. The operation is performed between ages 6 and 12 years, depending on
the firmness and development of the lower lateral
cartilages.

The most common secondary nasal deformity is
asymmetry of the nostrils: the nostril on the cleft
side is usually too large or too horizontal. Less frequent are nostrils that are too small. The ala may
be flat, and the base of the ala may be positioned
laterally and inferiorly as compared with the other
side. The nasal septum and columella may be deviated, and the nasal floor, absent. Correction of
the nasal deformity may involve several steps,
which I incorporate into one operation.
I use an external approach, which I described for
the first time in 1967. Over the years, I fine-tuned
the technique, allowing simultaneous lengthening
of the columella on the cleft side and exposure of
the lower lateral cartilages. I dissect the lower lateral cartilages from the skin and nasal mucosa and
reshape, restructure, and suture them together. It is
necessary to undermine and redrape the skin covering the nose, reposition the alar base, construct
the floor of the nose, close the nasolabial fistula,
perform septoplasty, and straighten the columella.
Occasionally simultaneous lateral and medial osteotomies may need to be performed.

Several techniques may be effectively used to correct the secondary cleft-lip/nasal deformity. These
extensive procedures involving the delivering and
reshaping of the entire lower lateral cartilages are
not attempted until the patient is age 7 years or
older, depending on the maturity of the nasal structures, primarily the development of the cartilages.
When correcting secondary nasal deformities,

special attention must be paid to the deviated septum and hypertrophy of the lower turbinate, both
of which may obstruct the nasal airway. I prefer to
perform septoplasty and turbinectomy at an earlier
rather than later age. After primary cleft-lip and
nose repair, as it is performed in our institution,
secondary cleft-lip/nasal deformities are minimal
to moderate in their severity.

PRIMARY BILATERAL CLEFT-LIP/NOSE
REPAIR: SALYER’S TECHNIQUE
I

Kenneth E. Salyer
The surgical technique for primary bilateral cleftlip/nose repair depends on the anatomy of the deformity. In cases of complete and partial bilaterally
symmetric cleft lip, alveolus, and palate, I close
both sides of the lip in one operation. I attempt
limited correction of the nasal deformity but leave
more radical nasal reconstruction for stage 2 repair
at a later age. In cases of asymmetric bilateral clefts
or in clefts with a displaced premaxilla, I use twostage cleft-lip repair. Closing the more affected

side stimulates growth of the prolabium, facilitating stage 2 of the operation, in which I close the
other side. During stage 2, I also attempt limited
correction of the nasal deformity by closing the
nasal floor and moving the displaced alar bases
medially.
Both of these techniques are followed by correction of the nasal deformity, which is performed at
age 1 year as stage 2 bilateral cleft-lip/nose reconstruction.

ASYMMETRIC BILATERAL CLEFT-LIP REPAIR:
NOORDHOFF’S TECHNIQUE
I

M. Samuel Noordhoff
In repairing the asymmetric bilateral cleft lip, the
particular surgical technique used depends on the
initial deformity. I apply certain principles in each
case to achieve a supple, symmetric, and functional lip. In my designs, which may be implemented in one or two stages, I aim to create an
arched, well-defined Cupid’s bow, a paralleling
white-skin-roll and red-line with intervening vermilion, a proportional philtrum, and symmetric
lateral segments of the lip. I pay particular atten-

tion to the reconstruction of the continuity of the
orbicularis oris muscle and buccal sulcus to make
sure the lip functions properly.
I use three basic concepts of reconstruction of
the central prolabial segment and free border of the
lip: straight-line closure, closure with lateral vermilion flaps, and reconstruction of a new Cupid’s
bow with lateral lip flaps pared from the free edge
of the lip, consisting of the orbicularis peripheralis
muscle, white-skin-roll, vermilion, and mucosa.

COORDINATED PRESURGICAL COLUMELLAR ELONGATION
AND ONE-STAGE PRIMARY NASAL CORRECTION OF THE
BILATERAL CLEFT LIP AND NOSE: CUTTING’S TECHNIQUE
I

Court B. Cutting
This section describes a method for primary correction of the bilateral cleft lip and nose that uses
presurgical nasoalveolar molding followed by primary lip and nose correction in a single surgical
stage. The procedure corrects the nasal-tip cartilage
deformity and elongates the columella without producing scars on the nose, columella, or central lip.
This procedure joins a new class of bilateral cleft
corrections in which the focus is placed primarily
on the deformity of the nasal-tip cartilage rather
than on skin maldistribution between the columella and prolabium. The usual model of bilateral
cleft repair corrects the shortage of columellar skin
by recruiting excess skin width from the prolabium
in some fashion. Use of the “skin paradigm” results
in a scarred columella that lacks cartilage support
and in tip cartilages whose domes remain widely
splayed apart. The secondary nasal deformity produced is difficult to correct.
The “cartilage paradigm” focuses primarily on
correction of the widely separated domes of the
lower lateral cartilages of the nose (McComb H,
1990; Trott JA, Mohan N, 1990; Cutting C, Grayson
B, 1993; Grayson B, Cutting C, 1993; Mulliken JB,
1995). In these repairs, the columellar skin shortage is corrected by recruiting skin from the nasal
tip or by ignoring the skin problem altogether, relying on the tip cartilages—when placed in the
right orientation—to correct the short columella as
the medial crura grow.

The repair described here incorporates another
step of presurgical nasal molding to elongate the
columellar skin and nasal lining. This is followed
by nose repair, which removes fat and fibrous tissue from between the domes through a retrograde
approach. The domes are then sutured together at
the midline to maintain a normal relation with
each other.

Figure 13-83 A: Grayson’s nasal alveolar molding.
The lateral palatal segments are positioned, and the
premaxilla is retracted by using a molding plate reinforced with external taping. Prongs from the front of the
molding plate press outward from inside at the nasal
tip. An elastic “saddle” is placed over the lip–columella junction to retract it. The two opposing forces
result in columellar elongation and in bringing the
dome cartilages together at the midline. B: The nasal
alveolar molding in place.

Figure 13-84 A: Bilateral asymmetric cleft lip, alveolus, and palate with a protruding premaxilla and very
small prolabium. B: Postoperative result 2 years after
surgery. Grayson’s orthopedic treatment preceded the
surgery in which Cutting’s technique was used. Note
the well-defined and symmetric lip and nose. C: The
same patient 2 years after surgery. Note the normal
shape of the nostrils, defined nasal tip, and long columella.

CORRECTION OF SECONDARY BILATERAL CLEFT-LIP AND
NASAL DEFORMITIES: BARDACH’S TECHNIQUE
I

Janusz Bardach

Primary cleft-lip/nose repair rarely results in a
normal-appearing lip and nose; further correction
is usually required. The most common secondary
lip deformities are deficiency of the vermilion in
the midportion of the lip, a short prolabium, an
asymmetrical vermilion, a prolabium that is too
wide, and a lip segment that is too long or too
short. While secondary cleft lip deformities vary in
type and severity, the nasal deformities, while
they may vary in severity, are usually uniform. The
columella is short, the nasal tip is flat or broad, the
nasal ala is flat, and the alar bases are displaced
laterally. The medial crura of the lower lateral cartilage are short and widely separated at the domes.
The lateral crura are flat, elongated, and may be Sshaped. The nostrils have a horizontal orientation.
In cases of a protruding premaxilla combined
with secondary lip and nasal deformities, I recommend that premaxillary repositioning be performed first, at 5 to 7 years of age. Correction of the
lip and nasal deformity is performed in one or
two operations, depending on the type and severity of the deformity.
In correction of the secondary bilateral cleft lip
and nasal deformities, a variety of techniques may
be used. I developed some of the techniques described in this chapter and presented them in

1991. In the majority of cases, I begin by re-operating on the patient—excising the scars on both sides
of the prolabium and using various transposition
flaps to create a normal-looking lip. I prefer to use
tissue from the upper lip itself rather than bring in
tissue from the lower lip, as is done in the Abbe
procedure. However, when the upper lip is tight,
the Abbe procedure is useful.
My original technique for correcting the bilateral
secondary nasal deformity was described for the
first time in 1967. The technique, fine-tuned over
the years, involves lengthening of the columella by
adding a skin flap from the upper lip, restructuring
the lower lateral cartilages by lengthening the medial crura, and shortening and reshaping the lateral crura. By suturing the medial crura at the appropriate height, a new nasal tip is created and the
nostrils are reoriented from a horizontal to an
oblique position. The alar bases are advanced medially, narrowing the nasal base. On rare occasions, a cartilage implant from the septum may be
needed to reinforce the medial crura and to solidify support of the nasal tip. I found that cartilage
implants were needed only when correction of the
secondary deformity was previously performed. I
prefer to correct the secondary lip and nasal deformity when the patient is aged 6 or 7 years.

CORRECTION OF SECONDARY BILATERAL CLEFT-NASAL
DEFORMITIES: SALYER’S TECHNIQUES
I

Kenneth E. Salyer
I use a number of different procedures for correction of the bilateral secondary cleft-nasal deformity, depending on the degree of the deformity
and the age of the patient. I frequently use early
repositioning of the alar cartilages in addition to
subtle columellar lengthening in patients age 3 to
6 years who show an inadequate columella length
in association with wide, flattened alar cartilages
and poor nasal-tip definition. Surgery at this age
results in less deformity and near-normal facial
growth. I use the same approach for primary bilateral deformity, except the alar cartilages are dissected, trimmed, and repositioned in a more extensive manner than is performed on the infant
during the first operation. This procedure can be
done at any age but is ideally performed before the
teen years; maturity of the alar cartilages is necessary so they can be dissected easily. In many cases
the columella skin is inadequate and needs lengthening and the alar cartilages need repositioning.
The lower lateral cartilages are displaced and exhibit abnormal growth, resulting in a broad alar
cartilage, which is flat instead of concave and contributes to the bilateral cleft-nasal deformity. The
nose is wide, flat, and lacks nasal-tip projection.
Correction of this deformity must be performed
while the patient is still growing. Although this is
not usually the definitive procedure, it allows
more normal growth to occur.
The most important reason for correcting the deformity at an early age is psychological, to lessen
the ridicule experienced by young patients. Therefore I operate early to achieve a near-normal position and shape of the nose and leave final correction for a later age. Trimming the broad alar
cartilage contributes to more normal nasal proportions during growth. Tip definition and columella
lengthening contribute to early correction of the
deformity. These reconstructed parts will grow in
a more normal fashion, decreasing the amount that
needs to be done in the final correction.
I consider the second correction of the nasal deformity to be the definitive procedure. I use a

semiopen approach, similar to that described in
chapter 11 for correction of the unilateral deformity. There are many variations of the approach,
but the columella skin must be adequate to allow
projection of the reconstructed nasal tip. If this approach falls short, then a method to lengthen the
columella, as described by Bardach elsewhere in
the chapter, is used.

FUNCTIONAL ANATOMY OF THE VELOPHARYNX
I

Hani Elkadi
I SOFT PALATE
The soft palate is a mobile musculomembranous
structure attached anteriorly to the hard palate and
blending laterally with the pharynx. It separates
the nasal cavity from the oral cavity and forms a
partial partition between the nasopharynx above it
and the oropharynx below it. With the exception of
the tensor veli palatini, all muscles of the soft
palate are innervated through the vagus nerve by
way of the pharyngeal plexus; the tensor is innervated by a branch from the mandibular nerve.

I VELOPHARYNX
The term velopharynx reflects the anatomic continuity between the palate and pharynx. It is a complex musculomembranous valve that surrounds
both the oropharyngeal isthmus and nasopharyngeal hiatus. The velopharynx includes the structures associated with the anterior and posterior
tonsillar pillars, as well as the soft palate and the
lateral and posterior walls of the oropharynx.
The action of the velopharynx in deglutition,
respiration, and phonation is characterized by two
basic sets of movements: elevation and depression
of the soft palate and movements of the lateral and
posterior pharyngeal walls.
The velopharyngeal musculature consists of several muscular slings formed by the following
paired muscles: the palatopharyngeus, palatoglossus, levator veli palatini, superior pharyngeal constrictor, and musculi uvulae. Associated with this
principal group of muscles are the salpingopharyngeus and palatothyroideus muscles (Figures
15-1–12B).
The palatopharyngeal and superior constrictor
slings form a complete ring around the nasal pharyngeal hiatus. The palatoglossal sling surrounds
most of the oropharyngeal isthmus.
The velopharyngeal muscles are responsible for

closing the nasopharyngeal isthmus during deglutition and speech. The isthmus is closed through
apposition of the velopharyngeal mucosa by elevation of the soft palate and inward movements of
the pharyngeal wall.
Functionally, the velopharyngeal muscles can
be divided into two groups: one that moves or alters the shape of the soft palate (the levator veli
palatini, palatoglossus, palatothyroideus, tensor
veli palatini, and musculi uvulae) and another
group that moves the lateral and posterior pharyngeal walls (the superior constrictor, palatopharyngeus, and salpingopharyngeus).

Palatine Aponeurosis
The palatine aponeurosis (Figures 15-1–3, 7, 12A
and B) is a sheet of fibrous tissue that constitutes
the principal structural element within the
velopharynx. It provides stability and flexibility to
the soft palate and serves as an anchoring point for
a number of its muscles. Along its attachment to
the hard palate, the palatine aponeurosis continues with the periosteum and submucosal connective tissue on the oral and nasal surfaces on the
hard palate. On either side, it continues around the
pterygoid hamulus with the tendon and fibrous
sheath of the tensor veli palatini. Posteriorly, it receives contributions from the internal pharyngeal
fascia, the salpingopalatine fascia, and the fascia of
Troltsch. Along the midline, it is attached to the fibrous sheaths of the paired palatal muscles: the levator, palatoglossus, palatothyroideus, and palatopharyngeus.

It is generally accepted that the levator veli palatini is responsible for moving the velum posteriorly and superiorly to contact the posterior pharyngeal wall during the production of oral speech

I VELAR LOWERING
Some authors attribute velar lowering to a cessation of levator veli palatini activity in combination
with the normal tendency of velar tissue to resume
its resting position. Other authors report that velar
lowering is caused by increased activity in the tensor veli palatini and palatoglossus muscles. Consistent with its anatomic orientation as antagonist
to the levator, the palatoglossus muscle appears to
be active in velar lowering, particularly in the production of nasal sounds and at the end of speech
(Figures 11A and 12B).

I MOVEMENT OF THE POSTERIOR
AND LATERAL PHARYNGEAL
WALLS
A solid understanding of the relative contribution
of the levator veli palatini and superior pharyngeal
constrictor muscles to the mesial motion of the lateral pharyngeal walls is yet to be achieved. Early
reports attributed this movement to the activity of
the salpingopharyngeal muscle. Recently on the
basis of clinical, radiologic, and anatomic observation, this movement was explained by the action of
the levator veli palatini. Some authors contended
that the superior pharyngeal constrictor muscle is
responsible for the movement of the lateral pharyngeal wall. The role of the horizontal fibers of
the palatopharyngeal muscle in the mechanism of
velopharyngeal closure requires further investigation. The anterior movement of the posterior pharyngeal wall is described subsequently.

Palatothyroideus/Palatopharyngeus Muscles
The common origin of the palatothyroideus and
palatopharyngeus (Figures 15-1–12B) muscles
consists of two heads: the anteroinferior head that

arises from the palatal aponeurosis and the posterior margin of the hard palate, and a posterosuperior head that arises farther back on the superior
surface of the palatal aponeurosis. The two heads
embrace the palatine part of the levator veli palatini. At the lateral border of the palate, the two layers merge before dividing into the palatopharyngeal muscle and the palatothyroideus muscle.
The term palatothyroideus muscle is used here
to describe the vertically oriented muscle fibers
running downward from the palate and inserting
into the posterior border of the thyroid cartilage.
The term palatopharyngeus muscle describes
the more horizontally aligned fibers running from
the soft palate in the posterior pharyngeal pillar to
the pharynx. These muscles are often referred to as
the “palatopharyngeal sphincter” or “Passavant’s
ridge” (Figure 15-12). It has been suggested that the
superior constrictor muscle contributes to the formation of the Passavant’s ridge.
In cleft palate, the major bulk of the palatopharyngeal muscle blends with the fibers of the levator to form “Veau’s cleft muscle.”

Palatoglossus Muscle
The palatoglossus muscle (Figures 15-1–6,
11A–12B) arises in the soft palate from the undersurface of the palatine aponeurosis and is inserted
into the side and dorsum of the tongue. It runs
downward in the anterior tonsillar pillar to interdigitate with the styloglossus muscle.
The direction of the fibers of the palatoglossus
muscle lies in an oblique plane that runs parallel
to the orientation of the levator’s fibers. Because
the fibers of both pairs of muscles converge at the
middle of the soft palate, each palatoglossus muscle is antagonistic to the contralateral levator muscle (Figure 15-11A and B).
The fibers of the palatoglossus muscle form two
bundles: an inferior bundle that passes into the
oral mucosa of the soft palate, and a superior bundle that pierces the levator sling and ends in connective tissue lateral to the musculi uvulae. Neither bundle reaches the midline or interdigitates
with the contralateral fibers. The palatoglossus
muscle elevates the posterior part of the tongue toward the palate and depresses the soft palate toward the dorsum of the tongue (Figure 15-12B).
In cleft palate, the palatoglossal muscle fibers
are underdeveloped. When identifiable, they are

Levator Veli Palatini
The levator arises from the rough quadrate area on
the inferior surface of the petrous portion of the
temporal bone in front of the carotid canal, the
carotid sheath, and part of the medial surface of
the cartilaginous portion of the auditory tube. It
forms a rounded belly that runs obliquely downward and medially above the upper border of the
superior pharnygeal constrictor to enter the soft
palate between the two layers of the palatopharyngeus muscle. Its fibers are inserted into the nasal
surface of the palatine aponeurosis and blend with
the muscle fibers of the opposite side, forming the
V-shaped levator sling that occupies the middle
portion of the soft palate. Contraction of the levator sling pulls the soft palate upward and backward, and the fibers attached to the auditory tubes
open them during deglutition. The levator veli
palatini is the principal mover of the soft palate
and the key muscle involved in elevating the pharynx. The muscle is innervated by way of the pharyngeal plexus by the inner branch of the vagus,
the fibers coming from the accessory nerve.
In cleft palate, the levator veli palatini fibers run
obliquely forward to join the palatopharyngeal
muscle, forming Veau’s cleft muscle, which is attached to the posterior edge of the hard palate and
the nasal periosteum (Figures 15-2, 4, and 9).

Tensor Veli Palatini
The tensor veli palatini muscle arises from the
scaphoid fossa, the lateral part of the medial pterygoid plate, and the spine of the sphenoid (Figures
15-1–12B). The major bulk of the muscle is attached to the lateral hook of the cartilage of the auditory tube and the fibrous tissue forming the
membranous portion of the auditory tube. It is supplied by a branch of the mandibular nerve through
the otic ganglion and a branch leading to the medial pterygoid muscle. It has been suggested that
the tensor is favorably situated to pull the palate
downward and forward. It acts largely in association with the tensor tympani in facilitating aeration of the middle ear.

Musculi Uvula
The musculi uvulae (Figure 15-3) arise by paired
slips from the nasal surface of the palatine aponeurosis on each side of the midline close to its attachment to the posterior border of the hard palate.
A distinct muscular slip reportedly arises from the
buccal surface of the palatine aponeurosis and
passes inferiorly to enter the ventral surface of the
musculi uvulae.
The muscular bellies pass over the levator sling
to enter the uvula where they terminate among its
glandular and connective tissue. Because little
nonmuscular tissue is present between the two
muscles, they have often been described as a single
muscle.
Recent studies have proposed that the musculi
uvulae act as a “flexible beam” or a pulling force
around a boundary. It may provide a stiffnessmodifying mechanism that resists the distorting
action of the levators on the curvature of the soft
palate in the coronal plane and straightens the
sagittal curvature of the soft palate during velar elevation. The functional roles of the musculi uvulae
in velar movement and phonation remain controversial, however.

TWO-FLAP PALATOPLASTY: BARDACH’S TECHNIQUE
I

Janusz Bardach
The main objectives of two-flap palatoplasty are
complete closure of the palatal cleft and the creation of an adequately functioning velopharyngeal
mechanism for normal speech production. The
main criteria for judging the success of palatoplasty are speech production and closure of the
cleft without oronasal fistulas.
There has been a widespread opinion that raising
mucoperiosteal flaps and exposing palatal bone inhibits maxillofacial growth. This assumption is
true for the Oxford technique, the von Langenbeck
technique, and techniques in which large areas of
palatal bone remain exposed after closure of the
palatal cleft. In contrast, two-flap palatoplasty, in
the majority of cases, allows complete closure of
the palatal cleft with minimal or no areas of denuded bone. Our long-term results in several clinical studies and in experimental studies in rabbits
and beagles have proven that two-flap palatoplasty
performed according to my principles and techniques does not inhibit maxillofacial growth.
I first described this technique in Polish in 1967
and further elaborated on it in the English literature in 1984, 1987, 1990, 1991, and 1995. Initially
I formulated the hypothesis that because of the
downward rotation of the mucoperiosteal flaps
into a more horizontal position, most palatal clefts
may be closed with little or no exposure of palatal
bone. This same principle applies to the construction of a drawbridge. Geometric analysis performed by Nosal and Bardach in 1985 confirmed
this hypothesis. It revealed that the initial slope of
the palatal shelf, angle of downward rotation of the
mucoperiosteal flaps, cleft width, and width of the
palatal shelves allow the palatal cleft to be closed
with minimal or no bone exposure. Tissue elasticity and the stretch–relaxation phenomenon also
aid in closing the palatal cleft.
Two-flap palatoplasty incorporates elements
from other surgical techniques, modifying the existing steps and adding new stages. In Surgical
Techniques in Cleft Lip and Palate (1991) I described the timing and principles of two-flap
palatoplasty, including geometric formulas used
for predicting the outcome of cleft-palate repair
and the possibility of complete or incomplete closure of denuded bone. The most visually detailed

presentation of the two-flap palatoplasty technique.
The most important innovations of the two-flap
palatoplasty technique include: atraumatic undermining of the mucoperiosteal flaps on the oral and
nasal sides; partial dissection of the neurovascular
bundles from the mucoperiosteal flaps to allow tension-free closure of the oral layer; precise dissection of the muscles of the soft palate from the nasal
periosteum and from the posterior edge of the hard
palate to allow appropriate repositioning of the
muscle sling; closure of the hard palate in two layers and suturing together of the oral and nasal layers to eliminate dead space between them and to
stabilize the mucoperiosteal flaps; and complete or
partial coverage of the denuded bone lateral to the
mucoperiosteal flaps on the oral side by the application of additional sutures.
I recommend performing two-flap palatoplasty
at age ⬃12 months. Clefts of the soft palate only or
of the soft and part of the hard palate may be successfully closed at age 9 months.
Evaluation of our late results of two-flap palatoplasty revealed an incidence of oronasal fistulas of
⬍3% and normal speech production in 80–85% of
patients.

SCHWECKENDIEK’S TECHNIQUE: TWO-STAGE
PALATOPLASTY
I

Janusz Bardach
Schweckendiek’s technique of two-stage palatoplasty also is known as primary veloplasty. The
concept, developed by Herman Schweckendiek,
was further advanced by his son Wolfram in Marburg, Germany. By repairing the soft palate at the
time of primary cleft-lip repair at age ⬃6 months
and leaving the hard palate open until age 12–14
years, the Schweckendieks strove to avoid midfacial growth aberrations, which they believed resulted from closure of the hard palate at an early
age. They were less concerned with speech results.
Our study in 1984 of the late results of two-stage
palatoplasty performed in Marburg on patients operated on by Wolfram Schweckendiek revealed
normal maxillary growth. However, the speech results showed an unusually high incidence of
velopharyngeal incompetence. Poor speech results
were also detected in patients operated on at the
University of Iowa Cleft Palate Center. Because of
these results, we abandoned the technique.
Variations of the technique are related to the
timing of hard-palate closure. I used two-stage
palatoplasty with some modifications, closing the
soft palate at the same time as cleft-lip repair, at
age 3 months. When deciding when to close the
hard palate, I followed the suggestions of experienced speech pathologists (Morris and Van Demark) and operated at age ⬃3 years because children were developing glottal stops and pharyngeal
fricatives. However, the main reason for poor
speech results in these patients was a short soft
palate caused by the technique of closing the soft
palate and leaving the hard palate unrepaired. Because of poor speech results, the technique was

abandoned at the University of Iowa Cleft Palate
Center.
However, two-stage palatoplasty remains popular in many centers, and therefore I describe it,
along with my modifications, in the following figures.
Figure 15-99 The operation begins with incisions on
the medial margin of the cleft in the posterior portion
of the hard palate. The incision is carried to the tip of
the uvula, revealing the muscles of the soft palate. The
next incision is carried laterally along the posterior
edge of the alveolar process and around the maxillary

Leonard T. Furlow, Jr.
Palate repair by double opposing Z-plasty closes
the soft palate by means of mirror-image Z-plasties

technique (initially without and later with intravelar veloplasty) decreased to 9% in 33 consecutive infants after double opposing Z-plasty repair.

operation results, complications, and variations
used in very wide clefts are described in more detail by Furlow (1986, 1990, 1991, 1992, 1995) and
Randall (1992). This technique has proven to be effective as a secondary procedure in the treatment
of VPI.

OXFORD TECHNIQUE OF CLEFT-PALATE REPAIR
I

Janusz Bardach
For many years, the Oxford technique was one of
the most widely used methods in cleft-palate repair. Many surgeons still use the procedure. This
technique includes elements designed by Wardill,
Kilner, and Peet. The Oxford technique also is
known as Wardill–Kilner–Peet palatoplasty or
three- or four-flap pushback palatoplasty. Threeflap palatoplasty is used in clefts of the palate
only, whereas the four-flap technique is used in
complete unilateral and bilateral palatal clefts associated with clefts of the lip and alveolus.
The Oxford technique emphasizes pushing back
the soft palate as the most important step to
achieve normal speech. The design of the mucoperiosteal flaps and their medial and posterior
transposition inevitably leaves large areas of bare
bone on both sides of the hard palate. Scarring in
these areas may lead to midfacial growth inhibition.
In the Oxford technique, the pushback of the
mucoperiosteal flap—and subsequently of the soft
palate—is achieved only on the oral side. To
achieve the same lengthening of the soft palate on
the nasal side, the nasal mucoperiosteum must be
cut along the posterior edge of the hard palate,
leaving a diamond-shaped raw area on the nasal
surface. Healing in this area by secondary intention leads to shortening of the soft palate due to
scar contracture.
Another shortcoming of the procedure lies in the
difficulty of closing the anterior palate. Failure to
achieve closure in this frequently results in
oronasal fistulas. The original Oxford technique
did not include closure of the nasal layer in the
hard palate. However, despite these shortcomings,
this technique became quite popular and is still
used in many countries.

The Oxford technique presented in the following illustrations includes my modifications: closing the nasal layer and suturing together the mucoperiosteal flaps on the oral side with those on
the nasal side to stabilize the oral flaps and avoid
dead space between the two layers, and releasing
the muscle attachment to the posterior edge of the
hard palate to create a muscle sling.

The success of palatoplasty is measured primarily
by the quality of speech. Of individuals with cleft
palate, 80–85% have normal speech after palate repair with two-flap palatoplasty. The success rate of
other techniques is reported to be 60– 90%. Impaired speech production, expressed by nasalization, requires further surgical intervention to improve the functioning of the velopharyngeal
sphincter, which is formed by the muscles of the
soft palate and by the superior constrictor muscle
of the pharynx. The inability of the velopharyngeal

sphincter to separate the oral pharynx from the
nasal pharynx results in hypernasality and nasal
air emission. Speech assessment by a speech pathologist, nasal endoscopy, videofluoroscopy, and
air-flow studies are all used to determine the necessity of performing pharyngoplasty. Various
techniques of pharyngoplasty have been developed, all with the aim of improving speech production by creating a better-functioning velopharyngeal mechanism.

Janusz Bardach
A protruding premaxilla with the maxillary segments positioned behind it presents a difficult
problem in terms of orthodontic and surgical treatment. Premaxillary repositioning is performed before a child enters school. The condition can be
treated with different orthodontic and surgical approaches. The most harmful approach, however, is
complete excision of the protruding premaxilla because this inhibits anteroposterior midfacial
growth. Working with Dr. William Olin, an experienced orthodontist, we developed a protocol for
the treatment of these patients.
In our approach, we do not use early orthodontic treatment to change the position of the premaxilla. The lip is repaired in two stages, between ages
3 and 6 months. The palate is closed, except for its
most anterior portion, at age 12 months. Maxillary
expansion begins between ages 4 and 5 years as
preparation for surgery. Surgical repositioning is
performed between ages 5 and 6 years. This procedure is combined with bilateral bone grafting by
using cancellous bone from the iliac crest.

Skeletal deformities associated with unilateral and
bilateral clefts require surgical treatment. On many
occasions, surgical treatment is combined with orthodontic treatment.
Orthognathic surgery is performed to correct
maxillary and/or mandibular skeletal deformities
associated with clefts. Impaired midfacial growth
and class III malocclusion appears as a secondary
deformity after cleft-lip and -palate repair. The optimal age for correcting maxillary deficiency is after the eruption of the secondary dentition at age
⬃14–16 years. The Lefort I procedure is most commonly used to correct the maxillary deficiency in
patients with clefts.

I introduced three variations to the LeFort I technique. I work with an orthodontist to plan this procedure, the orthodontist performs presurgical
treatment, and together we plan maxillary advancement and alignment in a new occlusion. Facial balance is enhanced by converting the straight
or concave profile to a convex profile. Skeletal correction not only improves facial appearance but
may also contribute to better speech and a more
open airway. More complete descriptions of the
correction of skeletal deformities are given in
chapters 7 and 8.

I LEFORT I MAXILLARY
LENGTHENING IN PATIENTS WITH
BILATERAL CLEFTS
Vertical deficiency of the maxilla and maxillary
retrusion frequently occur in patients with bilateral clefts. The maxilla may be lengthened by using one-piece or segmental maxillary advancement. To achieve optimal results, it is necessary to
determine the exact amount of maxillary lengthening necessary for the teeth to show during speaking
and smiling. Internal rigid fixation markedly minimizes vertical relapse. The best interpositional
graft material is, in my opinion, porous hydroxyapatite, which resorbs at a rate of 1% per year. With
internal rigid fixation, demineralized bone or autogenous bone grafts work equally well.
Figure 17-38 Maxillary advancement and lengthening is planned for patients with bilateral clefts
who have a vertically deficient and retruded maxilla. Preparation for this procedure includes orthodontic treatment and maxillary expansion. A
Lefort I osteotomy with interpositional bone grafting is followed by precise alignment of the dental
arches and leveling of all the teeth. The Lefort I os-

teotomy extends through the septum and anterior
maxillary wall to the pterygomaxillary region.
Figure 17-39 The maxilla is advanced. Occlusion is fixed with an acrylic wafer, which is prepared by performing surgery on the plaster models
and by using prediction tracings. The wafer is used
to fix the advanced and lengthened maxilla to the
mandible and remains in place for 6–8 weeks. After the maxilla is downfractured, an interpositional graft is placed to secure the permanent position of the lower segment. In my experience,
autogenous or demineralized bone grafts, as well
as hydroxyapatite, work well as interpositional
material with the use of rigid fixation.
Figure 17-40 The one-piece maxillary segment is
moved downward and secured with interpositional grafts and two titanium L-plates on each
side of the maxilla. The use of internal rigid fixation eliminates the need for long-term intermaxillary fixation.
