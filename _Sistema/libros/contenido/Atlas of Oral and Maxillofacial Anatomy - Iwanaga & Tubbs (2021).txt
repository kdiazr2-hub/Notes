Preface

This atlas focuses on the oral and maxillofacial regions and is relevant in the disciplines of oral
and maxillofacial surgery. The fine anatomy involved in oral and maxillofacial surgery has
progressed greatly in recent years, and therefore knowledge of anatomical structures relevant
to this field is now more important than ever. However, anatomical atlases that demonstrate the
newest anatomical findings in the oral and maxillofacial regions in a comprehensive manner
are scant. For oral and maxillofacial surgery, observing oral anatomy from the same view as
seen in clinical practice is critical for a better understanding of various invasive procedures.
However, to our knowledge, no other books have included high-resolution images of intraoral
anatomical dissections, especially using fresh cadavers. For example, the lingual nerve is a
structure that is always mentioned by mentors—“Be careful not to damage the lingual nerve as
it runs next to the lingual cortical plate.” Yes, we know that the lingual nerve is always at risk
when we make an incision in the retromolar to molar areas of the mandible; however, how
should we better minimize potential injury by an improved knowledge of this anatomy from an
intraoral, surgical view? The answer to this question and others is found throughout this atlas
via step-by-step fresh-cadaver dissections. We believe that this book will satisfy current needs
in the discipline of oral and maxillofacial surgery and is timely.

Anatomy of the Superficial Face

1.1

Superficial Face

Summary
The maxillofacial region has a rich blood and nerve supply.
The former derived from the external carotid artery and terminal branches of the internal carotid artery. The mimetic
muscles are innervated by the extracranial facial nerve

branches, and the sensation of the skin is supplied by primarily, trigeminal nerve branches. Some of these nerve branches
often communicate with adjacent nerves, e.g., marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve and mental nerve, although
the function of such connections is unclear. In this section,
the superficial structures of the maxillofacial region will be
demonstrated.

Bones of the Head and Neck

2.1

Overview (Skull)

Summary
The skull consists of 23 bones including the hyoid bone: eight
paired bones (temporal, zygomatic, parietal, maxillary, nasal,
lacrimal and palatine bones, and the inferior nasal concha) and
seven unpaired bones (occipital, frontal, sphenoid, ethmoid,

hyoid, mandible, and vomer). The vomer and inferior nasal
concha are within the nasal cavity. Three foramina (or notches)
of the face, e.g., supraorbital, infraorbital, and mental foramina,
are usually observed from an anterior view. A number of structures exist at the skull base as well as the cranial fossae, which
allows vessels and nerves to leave and enter the skull and also
act as the attachment of many skeletal muscles.

2.2

Sutures of the Skull

Bones of the Head and Neck

Metopic suture

Summary
The sutures of the skull are mostly paired except for the sagittal, median palatine, intermaxillary, and internasal sutures.
Many of these are still unfused at birth. For example, the
metopic (frontal) suture usually closes at the age of 3 months
to 2-years-old. Therefore, the metopic suture is usually not
observed in adults. Also, the incisive suture is usually still
open under the age of 12 years and is thus rarely visible in
the adult. Usually, the anterior and posterior fontanelles
close at the age of one and a half years old and three months
old, respectively. The coronal, sagittal, and lambdoid sutures
are still visible in the adult; however, some smaller sutures
are often completely fused in adults. Knowledge of these
sutures is vital to understanding clinical pathologies such as
craniosynostosis, hydrocephalus, fractures, and rapid maxillary expansion.

Mandibular Canal

Summary
The mandibular canal is a bony canal coursing inside the
mandible and it carries the inferior alveolar nerve and vessels.
The mandibular foramen is the entrance of the canal with the
lingula being located anteromedially and the ramus of the
mandible laterally. The main component of the canal is the
inferior alveolar nerve which is more than twice as large as
the vessels. The structure of the superior wall of the mandibular canal can change with tooth loss. When teeth are present,
the majority of the superior wall shows a trabecular or osteoporotic pattern, however, with tooth absence, a smooth-type
wall is seen. After entering the mandibular foramen, the mandibular canal runs anteroinferiorly and gradually approaches
the lingual cortical plate. When it reaches below the first
molar, the canal becomes closest to the lingual plate and then
suddenly changes its angle buccally and courses toward the
mental foramen with/without an anterior loop. Anatomical
variations of the mandibular canal will also be demonstrated.

Palate

Summary
The palate forms the roof of the oral cavity and is divided
into hard and soft palates. The hard palate is lined with keratinized and immobile mucosa. The soft palate is lined by
non-keratinized and mobile mucosa which forms the
entrance for the fauces between the oral cavity and oropharynx. The depth of the palatal mucosa, maxilla and palatine
bone forms the bony palate.
The palate is supplied by the greater and lesser palatine
arteries posteriorly, and sphenopalatine artery anteriorly.
Sensory and parasympathetic fibers are carried by the greater
and lesser palatine nerves posteriorly and the nasopalatine
nerve anteriorly. The palatine glands are minor salivary
glands that are mostly located bilaterally in the posterior palate. Greater palatine nerve blockade and other surgical techniques require detailed knowledge of the palatal area.

Summary
The floor of the mouth is considered a “dangerous” area for
dentists as it contains nerves and vessels which are not visible even on cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).
Also, non-malignant surgeries or even surgeries for cosmetic/functional reasons such as dental implant surgeries

can damage structures in the floor of the mouth. These iatrogenic injuries can potentially lead to life-threatening hemorrhage and permanent injury to sensory nerves. To avoid
these, a three-dimensional understanding of the anatomy of
the floor is necessary. In this section, the general anatomy of
the floor will be demonstrated. The course of many of these
nerves that are not visible on CBCT images can be predicted
if the anatomical relationships between the nerves and other

Lip

Summary
The lips are two folds that surround the oral orifice. On each side,
a labial commissure forms the angle (corner) of the mouth, usually near the first premolar tooth. The center of the upper lip has
a shallow vertical groove, i.e., the philtrum, which is bounded by
the columella of the nose superiorly. The lower lip has a small
depression in the midline that corresponds to the tubercle. The
junction between the hair bearing external skin and the hairless
red surface of the upper lip usually forms the so-called Cupid’s
bow (Standring 2015). The blood supply of the lip is provided
mainly by the facial artery and its branches. Skin sensation is
provided by the trigeminal nerve and the cervical plexus.
Histologically, the free external surface of the lips is covered by
a thin keratinized epidermis, which is continuous with the
mucosa at the vermilion (red) zone (Standring 2015). General
knowledge of lip anatomy is necessary for surgeries in and
around the lip such as cosmetic and reconstructive surgeries.

Temporomandibular Joint

Summary
The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is classified as a synovial joint that allows elevation, rotation, permits gliding, and
depression and normally functions in mastication, biting,
suckling, yawning, swallowing, and speaking. The mandibular fossa, the articular eminence of the temporal bone, and
the mandibular condyle all contribute to the TMJ. The articular surfaces of the TMJ are uniquely lined with fibrocartilage
rather than the normal hyaline cartilage. Its joint cavity is
divided into superior and inferior joint spaces by an articular
disc, and the disc attaches to the capsule. The main muscles
that allow for TMJ articulation are the masseter, temporalis,
and medial and lateral pterygoid muscles known as the muscles of mastication. From a clinical perspective, understanding the anatomy of the TMJ is significant for TMJ surgery,
diagnosis of temporomandibular joint disorders, and related
diseases and treatments.

Summary
The nose and nasal cavity consist of the frontal, nasal, maxillary, ethmoid, lacrimal, inferior nasal concha, palatine, and

ethmoidal cells) and nasolacrimal duct. The main blood supply is from the sphenopalatine branch of the maxillary artery
and anterior and posterior ethmoidal branches of the ophthalmic artery (a small area of the nostril is supplied by the
collumellar branch of the facial artery). The innervation is

Orbit

Summary
Vision is carried out by the optic nerve. To move the eyeball,
cranial nerves III, IV, and VI enter the orbit via superior
orbital fissure. Tears are produced in the lacrimal gland and
function to prevent dryness of the cornea. The excessive fluid

is collected by the medially located nasolacrimal duct to
drain into the inferior nasal meatus. The orbit receives arterial branches from the ophthalmic, facial, and transverse
facial arteries. Importantly, the superior and inferior ophthalmic veins drain into the cavernous sinus so that infections in
the orbit can affect the cavernous sinus. In this section, the
anatomy of the orbit will be shown.

Ear

Summary
The ear consists of external, middle, and internal parts. The
major function of the ear is hearing and balance. The external ear channels sound to the tympanic membrane. The middle ear, also known as the tympanic cavity contains the three

ear ossicles, i.e., malleus, incus, and stapes. The ossicles
transmit sound to the inner ear. The eustachian tube, originating from the tympanic cavity, connects to the nasopharynx and helps the ear drain fluid and equalizes air pressure.
The internal ear, the deepest part of the ear, has two different
structures and functions. The vestibule contributes to balance and the cochlea is the organ of hearing.

Larynx

Summary
The larynx is the airway that extends from the tongue to the
trachea, which open into the laryngopharynx and forms the
anterior wall of it. The larynx consists of five cartilages, i.e.,
epiglottic, thyroid, cricoid, corniculate, and arytenoid cartilages, and hyoid bone. Intrinsic muscles of the larynx are

innervated by vagus nerve branches, either external branch
of the superior laryngeal nerve or recurrent laryngeal nerve.
Dysfunction of the intrinsic muscles can result in hoarseness
or loss of tone. Internal branch of the superior laryngeal
nerve supplies the sensory fibers to the larynx. The larynx is
supplied by the superior thyroid branch of the external
carotid artery superiorly and inferior thyroid branch of the
thyrocervical trunk (branch of subclavian artery).

Summary
The pterygopalatine fossa (PT fossa) is bounded by the greater
wing of the sphenoid bone superiorly, pterygoid process posteriorly, and infratemporal surface of the maxilla anteriorly.
The infratemporal fossa (IT fossa) is situated medial and
inferior to the zygomatic arch and is not fully bounded by
bony structures. Its boundaries include the infratemporal
crest of the sphenoid bone superiorly, infratemporal surface
of the maxilla anteriorly, articular tubercle of the temporal
bone posteriorly, lateral plate of the pterygoid process medially, medial pterygoid muscle inferiorly, and ramus of the
mandible laterally.
The PT fossa is mainly related to V2 and its branches, and
the IT fossa to V3 and its branches. Both fossae are supplied
by the maxillary artery and its branches.

5.1

I ntracranial Course of the Cranial
Nerves

Summary
There are 12 pairs of cranial nerves. The olfactory (CN I) and
optic (CN II) nerves arise from the telencephalon and diencephalon, respectively, and the remaining cranial nerves
originate from the brain stem with the spinal accessory nerve
(CN XII) also arising partially from the cervical spinal cord.
The trochlear nerve (CN IV) arises from the dorsal aspect of
the midbrain and thus has the longest intracranial length of
all cranial nerves. The oculomotor nerve (CN III) exits the
midbrain in the crural cistern. The trigeminal nerve (CN V)
arises from the pons and the abducens (VI), facial (VII), and
vestibulocochlear (VIII) nerves arise from the pontomedullary junction. The glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX), vagus
nerve (CN X), cranial root of the accessory nerve (CN XI),
and hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) originate from the medulla
oblongata. In this section, the intracranial course of the cranial nerves will be shown.
