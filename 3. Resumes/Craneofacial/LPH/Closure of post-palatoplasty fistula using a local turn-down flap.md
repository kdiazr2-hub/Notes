## Closure of palatal fistula with turn-down flap

> [!info] Introducction
> Oral fistula is a complication following palatoplasty. It results in nasal emission during speech and deglution. 

Cohen classified fistulas into:

- Small: 1-2 mm
- Medium: 3-5 mm
- Large: >5mm

Pittsburg classified into:

- I: úvula
- II: Soft palate
- III: Soft and hard palate junction
- IV: Hard palate
- V: Primary and secondary palate junction
- VI: Lingual alveolar
- VII: Labial alveolar

The reported recurrence rate after surgical correction of fistula ranges 10-37%.

### Turn-down technique:

Under general anesthesia, injection of lidocaine with epinephrine in the area of the defect, incisions on the oral mucosa of the frontal and bilateral edges of this defect are made around the fistula, in the distal side of the fistula incision is made 3-5 mm longer than fistula size. This flap is turn-down and use to close the nasal side of fistula. The oral side of fistula is close with the two-flap procedure by bardach technique.

## Summary

The chapter discusses the treatment of postpalatoplasty fistulas, focusing on surgical techniques and recurrence rates.

## Introduction

- Fistula formation rates are reported to be between 0–76% after cleft palate repair.
- Factors influencing fistula rates include surgeons' experience, type of repair, cleft size, and timing of repair.
- Palatal fistulas can result in nasal emission during speech and deglutition.
- Fistulas are classified by size (small, medium, large) and localization (Pittsburgh Fistula Classification System).
- Recurrence rates after surgical correction range from 10–37%.

## Materials and Methods

- Three patients with postpalatoplasty fistulas in the hard palate (PFCS-4) were treated.
- The surgeries were performed under general anesthesia, using a local turn-down flap technique.
- Incisions were made around the fistula on both oral and nasal mucosa sides.
- One case was the first repair, while another was the second repair in one patient.
- The oral side of the fistula was closed with the two-flap procedure by Bardach technique.

## Results

- All postoperative wounds were covered initially.
- Postoperative follow-up was conducted between 7 and 14 days after surgery.

## Discussion

- The timing of fistula treatment can vary, with smaller fistulas potentially closing spontaneously over time.
- Medium or large-sized fistulas require earlier closure to prevent food regurgitation and nasal twang in speech.
- Several methods exist for correcting postpalatoplasty fistulas, including the use of local turn-down flaps.

## Correction Methods

- Small defects may close spontaneously.
- For small defects, bone grafting can be used with a double-layer closure.
- Longitudinal defects require modification of the von Langenbeck procedure.
- Large defects might need pharyngeal flap repair.
- Local and buccal flaps are considered for larger defects.

## Recurrence Rates

- Recurrence rates range from 10% to 37%
- Cohen et al. reported a recurrence rate of 37% in their study.
- Muzaffar’s paper indicated a recurrence rate of 10%.
- Landheer et al. reported only 9% recurrence rate.

## Two-Layer Method

- Simpler than tongue and buccal flap methods.
- More effectively uses bilateral mucosal tissues for closure.
- Recommended for large palatal fistula correction.

## Key takeaways
- Fistula formation rates vary widely after cleft palate repair.
- Surgical methods include local turn-down flaps for closure.
- Recurrence rates range from 10% to 37%, with some studies reporting lower rates.
- The two-layer method is recommended due to its simplicity and effectiveness.
- Local and buccal flaps are used for larger defects, while bone grafting can be used for smaller ones.
- Timing of treatment varies based on fistula size.
